\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Cognitive and adaptive radar}
Cognitive radar frames the sensing loop as an adaptive decision process with feedback from the receiver to the transmitter \cite{haykin2006cognitive}. The fully adaptive radar (FAR) framework formalized perception--action cycles for detection and tracking \cite{bell2014far,bell2015jstsp}, and later work developed cognitive resource management from adaptive scheduling toward learned allocation \cite{charlish2020development,martone2021waveform}, including game- and bandit-based approaches to radar coexistence and adversarial settings \cite{martone2018spectrum,howard2022bandits}. These lines establish that a radar's transmit behavior should adapt to its environment, but the environment is usually non-adaptive or drawn from a fixed distribution; the opponent does not learn against the radar. Reinforcement learning has likewise been applied to phased-array beam and dwell scheduling under overload \cite{kosuru2022qlearn}, again with scripted demand. Our benchmark keeps this resource-management structure---services, bearings, deadlines, and a finite activation budget---but places a learning jammer team on the other side of the link.

\subsection{Learning in radar--jammer and anti-jamming games}
A growing literature learns jamming and anti-jamming decisions as games. Multi-agent deep RL has been used for cognitive-radar anti-jamming decision-making \cite{jiang2023dsp,wang2024ietrsn} and for multi-jammer radar countermeasures \cite{xie2023multijam}; the wireless anti-jamming literature is surveyed in \cite{jia2025survey}, with deep-RL power and channel defenses dating back to two-dimensional jamming avoidance \cite{han2017twodim}. Neural fictitious self-play provides a standard solution concept for such imperfect-information games \cite{heinrich2016deep}. The dominant endpoints in this literature are link-level quantities: SINR, detection probability at the current dwell, or a per-step utility. FluxPhased complements this line with a mission-level endpoint: a mission succeeds only when the correct service detects the correct bearing within a deadline window, so per-step link wins do not immediately translate into task denial. This distinction is what makes containment---the fraction of adversarial marginal disruption that a defense removes---measurable at all.

\subsection{Multi-agent reinforcement learning and self-play}
Our trainer combines standard components: PPO with clipped surrogate objectives \cite{schulman2017proximal}, parameter-sharing multi-agent actors with centralized critics in the CTDE family \cite{yu2022surprising,lowe2017maddpg,foerster2018coma,rashid2018qmix,dewitt2020ippo}, and self-play \cite{heinrich2016deep,vinyals2019alphastar,berner2019dota}. The literature is explicit that learning with a learning opponent creates non-stationarity and opponent-specialization problems \cite{hernandezleal2017survey,foerster2018lola} and that differentiable games can have cyclic or non-transitive dynamics whose equilibria require game-theoretic rather than purely gradient tools \cite{balduzzi2019mechanics,lanctot2017psro}. Population and league methods address this by diversifying opponents \cite{vinyals2019alphastar,lanctot2017psro}; role-oriented and macro-strategy methods structure within-team coordination \cite{wang2020roma,wu2018moba}. We deliberately use none of these stronger machineries: the S7 game converges under plain two-team self-play, which isolates attacker-count scaling from league effects, and R5 tests only a minimal opponent-class mixture. The singleton-vulnerability result of Section~V shows why opponent-population questions remain relevant for this benchmark even after convergence.

\subsection{Position of this work}
FluxPhased sits between the radar resource-management community and the adversarial-learning community. Its action space is link-facing (cells, beams, services), but its reported endpoint is task completion under service, bearing, deadline, and energy constraints. This enables a question that neither link-level anti-jamming studies nor single-sided scheduling studies answer: whether an apparent defensive advantage survives attacker-count scaling, and whether the resulting two-team equilibrium is stable or cyclic. The comparison is built around three controls---the idle-radar floor, a scripted-radar jammer view, and a co-located-jammer ablation---plus evaluation-only scripted baselines (random, greedy, sweep, and stare policies) that anchor the learned policies against non-learning behavior. The controls separate absolute loss, adversarial marginal loss, equilibrium stability, robustness to a changed opponent class, and the distinction between an equilibrium policy and a mission-greedy policy.

Acceptance testing of multifunction phased-array radar networks currently lacks a measurement instrument for learned, numerically scalable jamming threats: link-level signal-to-interference ratios cannot reveal whether a network under adaptive electronic attack still completes its deadline-constrained mission load. We address this gap with a task-level adversarial test-and-evaluation protocol, FluxPhased, that couples deadline-constrained mission scheduling to bearing-aware planar-array link physics, and use it to measure how the radar--jammer balance shifts when the attack is scaled up. Missions carry service and bearing identities, deadlines, and exact detection credit; the link model combines generalized array-factor responses with finite jammer energy budgets. Three protocol components are independently reusable: a pre-training contestability gate that rejects degenerate physical regimes before any training, a floor-adjusted containment metric $\eta$ that separates natural scheduling loss from adversarial loss, and evaluation-only scripted anchors that expose opponent specialization in both teams.

The system-level finding is stated in engineering terms: with the total jammer activation budget held fixed at 63 cells per episode, distributing the attack across two spatially separated jammers reduces the fraction of adversarial mission denial that learned radar scheduling removes by roughly two-thirds. In the one-jammer versus two-radar game, the valid 12-dB S6 runs neutralize $62.8\%\pm1.6\%$ of the jammer's floor-adjusted disruptive power across three training seeds. When the attacker team is doubled under the same budget, three converged seeds yield a stable 2v2 equilibrium with head-to-head mission drop $0.3366\pm0.0143$ and neutralization $23.0\%\pm1.1\%$. Radar competence against idle jammers remains low, showing that the degradation is an offensive scaling effect rather than a corresponding defender collapse. A three-seed co-located-jammer control attributes most of the change to attacker count under the S7 separated-radar geometry (neutralization $26.6\%\pm2.9\%$), with cross-fire geometry contributing a further reduction to $24.2\%$. Evaluation-only scripted baselines show that both learned teams dominate random and fixed-stare policies, yet a greedy mission-stare radar holds drop at $0.0889$ against every trained jammer team, so head-to-head values are equilibrium properties of the co-adapted pair rather than performance ceilings; jammers co-trained against that stare recover part of the penalty (drop $0.213$), confirming the margin is strategic rather than physical. Finally, a lightweight opponent-class mixture reduces singleton relative leverage from $0.502$ to $0.262$ at 75\% singleton exposure, with the largest neutralization value at 50\% exposure. The protocol, containment metric, and pre-training gate together form a test-and-evaluation procedure that is applicable to learned radar resource management before any fielded deployment, and that establishes a measurable separation between equilibrium stability, attack scaling, and opponent-distribution robustness.
